How to print and put your poster together

  1. Print at 100%. In the print dialog choose "Actual size", "100%" or "No scaling". "Fit to page" shrinks every sheet a little, and then the tiles no longer match. Print one page first and check that nothing is clipped at the edges; if it is, raise the printer margin and download again.
  2. Lay the sheets out. With page labels on, each sheet has its row letter and column number in the bottom margin (A1 is top left, A2 is to its right, B1 is below it), and the assembly map page shows the whole layout.
  3. Trim. On every sheet except the ones in the first column, cut off the left margin along the cut marks. On every sheet except the top row, cut off the top margin. A craft knife and a metal ruler give a straighter edge than scissors.
  4. Overlap and glue. Lay each trimmed sheet over its neighbour so the cut edge sits on the overlap marks of the sheet underneath; the strip in between shows the same part of the image twice, so it hides small errors. Join each row first with a glue stick or tape on the back, then join the rows.
  5. Finish the edges. Trim the outer margins last, or leave them as a white border. With Fit, trim off any blank paper on the last row or column.

How many DPI does a big poster need?

DPI (dots per inch) here means how many of your image's pixels land on each inch of paper. A 6000-pixel-wide photo printed 20 in wide gets 300 DPI; printed 60 in wide it gets 100 DPI. What matters is whether anyone can see the pixels, and that depends on how far away they stand. People with normal eyesight resolve detail down to about one arcminute, which works out to roughly 3,438 ÷ distance in inches:

Viewing distanceDPI where pixels stop being visible
30 cm (1 ft), in your handabout 290
60 cm (2 ft)about 145
1 m (3 ft 3 in)about 90
2 m (6 ft 6 in), across a roomabout 45
3 m (10 ft)about 30

So a wall poster at 100 DPI looks fine from a metre away, and the tool warns you below that. Under 100 DPI it can still look good from across a room, but sharp edges and text will look soft up close. Making the poster smaller, or starting from a bigger image, raises the DPI. Upscaling a small image with an AI upscaler before you tile it can help a lot with photos.

The PDF never stores more than 300 DPI per tile, because home printers can't show more and the file would only get bigger. Images with transparency are kept as PNG tiles; everything else uses high-quality JPEG.

Fit or fill?

Fit prints your whole image at exactly the width you asked for. Because paper comes in whole sheets, the last column or row usually has some blank paper that you trim off. Fill enlarges the image a little so it covers every sheet completely, cropping an even strip from the edges that stick out. Use Fill when you want no wasted paper and don't mind losing a little of the edges.
